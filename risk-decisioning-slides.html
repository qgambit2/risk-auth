Systems Design | Eugene Bendersky

Real-Time Payments Risk Decisioning at Intuit

Deciding, during payment authorization, whether each transaction proceeds, is blocked, or goes to human review. Owned as tech lead (4 engineers) on the payments risk team.

300M+transactions decisioned per year
$300B+annual payment value on the platform
$5B+/yrfraud losses prevented (system contribution)
Hard SLAverdict inside the authorization budget
  • The problem: fraud decisions need 100+ data points and rich rules, but the payment platform gives risk a fixed slice of the authorization budget. Accuracy and latency pull in opposite directions.
  • The users: the payment platform calling in-line, risk analysts authoring rules that must ship same-day, and fraud operations working the ambiguous middle.
US Patent 11,869,008: ML plus configurable rules for payment fraud scoring (co-inventor)1 / 3

Architecture: one transaction through the system

Spring Boot on AWS/Kubernetes, 40 pods serving production; RDS PostgreSQL, DynamoDB, SNS/SQS2 / 3

Decisions, trade-offs, outcomes

1. Overlap, don't prefetch

  • Problem: fetch-then-evaluate with 100+ data points could not make the SLA.
  • Chosen: every fetch fires as a Future at once; rules block only on values they touch.
  • Rejected: a prefetch cache (stale fraud signals are a risk decision) and fewer data points (accuracy pays for the platform).
  • Moved the decision store and short-term aggregates from self-managed Cassandra to DynamoDB: Cassandra's TP99 tail was hurting the hot path; DynamoDB fixed it, at the price of Spark paying per read. Long-running aggregates came from a partner team's always-on streaming Spark, written to a DynamoDB in another AWS account and served to us by Nighthawk, a partner service the data layer calls like the model scoring service.

2. Rules plus ML, not either

  • Rules: analysts respond to new fraud patterns same-day. Artifacts ship through an artifact manager the engine polls every minute, no redeploy, and declare the data a rule flow needs as annotations on their Java classes; the service turns those into the fetches. Every verdict carries its trace.
  • Ground truth closes the loop: the processor can still decline what risk approved; PayAuth publishes the outcome to SNS, Risk Data Tools reads it from a subscribed SQS queue and writes it into the decision record, so labels and backtests see what actually happened.
  • ML: a separate scoring service, called by the data layer with the merchant key and transaction fields; it keeps its own per-merchant aggregates and its models train on the same decision data we publish. Its score is one more input to the rules.

3. Rollout safety as architecture

  • Shadow validation before promote: every prod decision is published with its rule data; Decision Data Tools replays it against the candidate artifact in staging, same data, no refetch, and compares verdicts.
  • Sticky per-merchant A/B for the live rollout: a new rule runs for a set percentage of merchants, and the assignment is deterministic, so a merchant lands in the same bucket every time.
  • ActiveMQ to Kafka, separate topics for decisions, rule violations, and policy: replay and fan-out without coupling the consumers (validation, case management, a partner team's streaming aggregates).
  • Fail open: if the risk service is down, transactions still process. The cost is a window of higher fraud exposure; the alternative was stopping payments.
Outcomes: SLA held at 300M+ transactions/year; system contributed to $5B+ per year in prevented fraud losses; design survived a full ownership handoff to a new team, which runs it today.
